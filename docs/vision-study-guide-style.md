# Pass B study-guide editorial style

**Owner approved:** 2026-09-03
**Purpose:** Golden voice and question-selection reference for the player-facing Pass B study guide.

This document controls editorial style, not factual truth. B1-B3 evidence and source-bound
research remain authoritative for each work. Never copy facts from the Julius Caesar example
into another record.

## Audience and editorial standard

Write for readers who can comfortably handle SAT/college-entry prose and have some
familiarity with art, but are not specialists.

The division of labor is:

> Hotspots teach what to notice. The study guide explains why those details matter,
> what larger traditions they belong to, and what genuinely interesting questions the
> object raises.

- Be specific and intellectually serious without becoming academic or inaccessible.
- Use a specialist term when it adds real precision, then explain it naturally.
- Ask questions a curious, attentive museum visitor might genuinely have.
- Prefer answers that change how the reader understands the work and connect it to a
  broader artistic practice, historical problem, or interpretive question.
- Do not ask readers to identify something visually self-evident.
- Do not restate a hotspot as a redundant question.
- Preserve strong legacy questions and improve their answers when they are already the
  best teaching structure.
- Include biography, birthplace, routine provenance, acquisition history, or catalog
  dating only when it materially changes the interpretation.
- Keep citations in internal `sourceRefs`; do not repeatedly name sources in player copy.
- Select the strongest five to seven questions. Do not promote every researched fact.
- Reject a candidate question whose answer is merely correct rather than illuminating.

## Distilled house style (from the north stars)

These are the recurring structural moves that make the owner-selected records work. They
describe HOW a strong guide reads, not facts to reuse. They operate within the five-to-seven
question budget above; they do not raise it.

1. The `why` is one sentence that fuses date, place or culture, medium, and the single most
   recognizable visual giveaway. Open by naming what instantly identifies the work, not with
   generic praise.
2. Cues are arrow inferences: `visible feature → what it tells you` (e.g. "red figures on a
   glossy black ground → Athenian red-figure"). A cue teaches the reading move, pairing an
   observable trait with the conclusion it licenses.
3. Within the selected questions, run a deliberate arc: the early questions decode THIS work
   (who, what, where, iconography); the later ones generalize into portable connoisseurship
   (how to date it, why this medium, how to tell the movement apart). The transferable half is
   the payoff the owner values in *The Ambassadors*.
4. Prefer the transferable pair whenever attribution is teachable: "how can we tell this is by
   X from the work itself?" immediately followed by "how do I spot X elsewhere?" Both force the
   answer to name style markers (contour, handling, palette, composition) rather than facts a
   player would have to already know.
5. Answers teach reading-the-evidence: "Look for…", "Notice…", "point toward…", grounded in
   what is on the surface. Identification is derived from visible traits, not asserted.
6. Teach technique as meaning, never as a spec: connect the medium to its visible effect ("oil
   lets him blend edges into shadow"), not a dry materials label.
7. Each answer is self-contained (roughly two to four sentences) and re-explains its own terms.
   A player can read any single question and answer cold.
8. Hedge honestly, phrased around the object: "may suggest", "often linked to", "a town like
   Delft" rather than over-asserting. Uncertainty is interpretive caution, not a data gap.
9. Preserve the mystery. A strong guide resists flattening the work to one tidy lesson.
10. Notes are distinct spatial where-to-look captions (a `[head]` locating a region, a body
    explaining it), walking the eye across the picture. Do not recycle cue or guide text as notes.

Two object-level hazards the north stars also surface:

- Do not anchor a "key visible clue" on a feature that may not actually be present in the shown
  image (the *Meleager* boar's-head risk). If identification leans on it, B1/B3 must locate it
  first.
- Keep atmosphere and interpretation tethered to visible evidence, and hedge figure activity
  that is not clearly legible (the *Little Street* overclaim risk). Do not substitute poetic
  flourish for observation.

## Golden example: Mino da Fiesole, *Julius Caesar*

This example establishes the desired voice, difficulty, specificity, and question
selection. It is not a reusable content template.

### Did Mino da Fiesole carve the entire relief himself?

Not quite. The portrait panel is attributed to Mino, while the garland is attributed to
his workshop. You can see why scholars separate them: the portrait is deeply and
precisely carved in smooth white marble, while the gray limestone garland is shallower,
rougher, and more broadly handled. The contrast offers a glimpse of how Renaissance
workshops divided labor while producing a visually unified object.

### Is this actually a portrait of Julius Caesar from life?

No—it is a 15th-century imagining made roughly 1,500 years after Caesar's death. Mino
probably used ancient coins from the Medici collection as his starting point, adapting
their profile portraits into sculpture. For Renaissance patrons, an antique-style image
of Caesar could celebrate the classical past while also advertising their own learning
and cultural prestige.

### What does *all'antica* mean, and how do you see it here?

*All'antica* means "in the antique manner": a Renaissance work deliberately made using
the visual language of ancient Greece or Rome. Here, that language appears in the
coin-like profile, Latin inscription, classical drapery, carved marble, and presentation
of Caesar as an exemplary ancient figure. The object is not Roman, but it wants you to
think about Rome.

### How can we date it to the mid-1400s in Florence?

The crisp classical bust set in an arched frame with a fruit-and-ribbon garland is a
hallmark of the Florentine Renaissance revival of antiquity. Its carving and design
resemble Mino's other Florentine works from before his first extended stay in Rome around
1459. Those comparisons help scholars narrow the date to approximately 1455-1460: the
visible style establishes the broader context, while Mino's career provides the tighter
range.

### Why is Caesar shown in strict left-facing profile rather than three-quarter or frontal view?

The pose recalls Roman coins and carved gems, where rulers were commonly presented in
profile. Mino probably studied examples in the Medici collection, transforming a
familiar ancient format into a much larger marble image. The pose is therefore a status
statement and a learned historical reference as much as a stylistic choice.

### What do the incised letters IV and CE flanking the head mean?

They abbreviate *IVLIVS CAESAR*, identifying the sitter. The format—portrait image paired
with a naming inscription—echoes Roman commemorative practice and ancient coin legends.
Renaissance humanists favored this combination to display classical learning and to
associate their patron's environment with the prestige of antiquity.

### What was the original function and setting of this relief?

The relief sits within a large limestone block, suggesting that it was originally
embedded in a wall, perhaps in a lunette above a doorway. In that setting, Caesar's image
would have been part of the building's public identity rather than an isolated sculpture
viewed at eye level. Its classical subject could lend the surrounding architecture an
air of authority, learning, and historical continuity.
